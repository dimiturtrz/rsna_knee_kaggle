# Q-V reply — units, on-disk substrate, and the cheapest experiment

You are right about the unit. The thresholds in the literature are **images / slices**, not exams or studies.

## 1. The thresholds are per-image

* Ilse et al. (Data Scaling Laws for Radiology Foundation Models, 2025) pretrain on 30k–3.5M **chest X-ray images** (not studies). They write "as few as 30k in-domain samples" and "100k samples" for RAD-DINO to surpass open-weights.
* MedDINOv3 (Li et al., 2025) pretrains on **3.87M axial CT slices** and reports +1.07% DSC.
* So the comparison unit for a 2D slice encoder is slices.

## 2. What is actually on disk

I counted it:

* **KMAR-50K**: 3,088 NIfTI volumes / **71,622 slices**, 67 GB, CC BY 4.0. This is the only large, clean, already-downloaded unlabeled knee-MRI substrate.
* **fastMRI singlecoil train**: only a 14 GB `.part` file of the 89 GB archive — not usable yet.
* **fastMRI knee DICOM release**: not the 120 GB you assumed. Only `knee_mri_clinical_seq_batch2` is present, and it contains **96 files / 14 MB**, basically a one-patient sample. Batch1 is missing.

So the on-disk substrate is ~72k slices, not several hundred thousand. That puts us between MI2's 30k crossing point and RAD-DINO's 100k crossing point.

## 3. Q-V answers

1. **Small-end published recipe?** Yes — Ilse et al. ran the 30k/50k/100k continual pretraining subsets on 32 H100s for 0.33/0.58/1.17 hours, i.e. roughly **10–37 GPU-hours** per run. For the DINOv2-family (RAD-DINO), 100k images was the point where continuation beat open-weights. No positive DINO-family result below 100k images is reported in that paper.

2. **Crossing point as a function of target size?** Not in Ilse. Their target task sizes are fixed (2M images for findings classification, etc.). I did not find a published ablation that varies both substrate size and target-task size. The Zhang et al. 200-vs-800-subject row you mention would need to be checked separately; I have not located it yet.

3. **Cheapest proxy?** A frozen linear probe on the target task is exactly what Ilse uses to measure scaling. Train the head on frozen features from the open-weights checkpoint, then train it again on frozen features from the continued checkpoint, and compare. No end-to-end fine-tuning. That is the experiment to run first.

4. **Honest negative in image units:** VinDR is 9k CXR images. There, RAD-DINO open-weights beats the 30k–100k continued checkpoints (reversed scaling / catastrophic forgetting), and MI2 needs **>>100k** images to beat open-weights. Our holdout/target is smaller than VinDR in images, so the reversal risk is real and not just a theoretical caveat.

## 4. The cheapest way to decide the lane

There are two cheap experiments, not one:

* **Use OrthoFoundation-L.** I downloaded its released `OrthoFoudation-L.pth` (1.2 GB, DINOv3-L continued on 1.25M knee images, MIT license). If it loads through our DINOv3-L encoder path, we get the answer to "does knee-specific continuation help?" without running SSL ourselves. Bead `knee-440m` tracks this.
* **Run a frozen linear-probe comparison** on our 909-study holdout using (a) raw DINOv3-L features vs (b) OrthoFoundation-L features. This is the proxy from the scaling literature and costs no card time beyond feature extraction.

If OrthoFoundation-L does not move the linear-probe AUC, the continuation lane is closed at our scale. If it does, we can decide whether to mount it and train a member.
